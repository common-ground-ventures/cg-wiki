Permitting a category that did not exist yet

I wanted to open Southern California's first axe-throwing venue, in a Costa Mesa warehouse I already had, but the city's use code had never contemplated the category. There was no existing permit path to follow.

Instead of waiting for the category to become established somewhere else first, I pursued a first-of-its-kind use permit directly, building the case for why the use fit inside a code that didn't name it.

Being early to a category means the rulebook hasn't caught up yet. The choice is either wait for someone else to set the precedent, or be the one who does the work of proving the case to the regulator. I chose the second, same instinct that made me an early mover on green building and on search marketing.

Costa Mesa approved the use permit. The venue opened as one of the first of its kind in the region, inside a warehouse I converted myself, generated nearly $89,000 in sales in its first four months from a standing start, and the business sold within about a year of opening.

When a category doesn't have a permit path yet, someone has to be willing to build the case for the regulator instead of waiting for the rules to catch up on their own.

This required convincing a room of public officials, unfamiliar with the category, to say yes to something they'd never had to evaluate before. A negotiation built on preparation and credibility rather than precedent.
