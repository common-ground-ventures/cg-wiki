Silver Mountain Ranches: the deal review before the investor does

Work · Capital

A full Common Ground consulting engagement on a 344-acre Arizona homesite community: the model, the lender and investor case, the buyers, water and approvals, and the brand, reviewed the way the money will review it.

Wiki/Work/Silver Mountain Ranches

What it is

Silver Mountain Ranches is a 344-acre homesite community in the Bradshaw Mountains foothills of Yavapai County, Arizona, put together by its owner over about twenty-five years. The plan is 40 homesites of five to ten acres, nine permitted wells, off-grid homes, and public land on three sides.

The owner asked Common Ground into every part of the project: the financials and the model, the lender package, the investor raise, the buyers, the approvals and the brand. Common Ground's job is to make the project read as well to the people paying for it as the land does to anyone who walks it.

What Common Ground did

  • Common Ground's starting point was a simple one. Any serious investor or lender will run their own review of the deck before they take a meeting, so the useful thing is to run that review first, on the owner's side of the table, while there is still time to answer the questions in the deck.
  • The first round, in early October 2026, read the investor deck the way an outside investor would and returned its notes as the questions that investor would ask, each with what to consider doing about it.
  • The second round ran the full Common Ground deal review in six stages. The forecast was rebuilt from the spreadsheet and run month by month the way a construction lender will. Seven reviewers each read it from one seat: the equity investor, the construction lender, the family buying a home, water and entitlements, building cost and schedule, appraisal and absorption, and brand and story. A red team attacked the deal and the other reviewers. An independent second check went over every number, a final reviewer threw out anything that did not trace to a slide, a cell or a public source, and Common Ground checked the statutes itself.
  • That last stage changed a finding. Several reviewers read Arizona's water adequacy rule as a possible stop on lot sales. The statute text says otherwise for this county, which has not adopted the mandatory rule: a water finding is disclosed to buyers rather than blocking the subdivision, and refundable lot reservations are allowed before the public report is issued. The owner got a legal way to show demand early instead of a red flag that was not real.
  • The notes covered how the homes get funded while they are being built, how investors are paid before sales arrive, a downside case that tests slower sales and longer approvals, the full approval path, what buyers will ask, the build cost behind each plan, and a storyline that leads with the land.

Results

  • The owner took the first round: the revised deck nine days later carried three scenarios side by side, a real risks slide, the full well table and the amenities budget inside the model.
  • The second round went to the owner and his partner in October 2026, with every finding paired with a fix and the documents that would let the review go further.
  • Nothing has closed. The engagement continues on a verbal basis, and the next version of the deck goes through the same review.
