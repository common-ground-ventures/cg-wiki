Save the World Air: testing a hard technical claim from inside the company

Work · Capital

A consulting seat at a small public pipeline-flow technology company in Santa Barbara, around 2011: testing a hard claim from close range.

Wiki/Work/Save the World Air

What it is

Save the World Air, Inc. was a small, thinly traded public company in Santa Barbara whose technology was meant to improve how oil moves through pipelines. It was raising money on a technical story that could not be checked from a filing. In August 2015 it renamed itself QS Energy.

Around 2011 Fowler took a consulting seat there, paid in equity, a personal seat held while he ran Tellus Design and Build.

What Common Ground did

  • Fowler took a seat inside the company rather than reading it from a distance through its filings, to hear what its own people believed rather than what it published.
  • He asked for the claim as a mechanism: what the technology physically did to the oil, the pipe or the flow, and what pipeline operators already pay for to get the same result.
  • He asked who besides the company was paid to say it worked, and whether any outside lab or pipeline operator had put money or a test bay behind it.
  • He helped the company raise about $3 million, and kept the raise separate from the question of whether the technology worked, since capital raised measures persuasion, not physics.
  • He named the cost of the seat plainly: a consultant with equity in the company he is testing judges more gently than a stranger would, and the only cure is a written finding someone who was not in the room could check.
  • He read the later name change as an announcement, not a finding about the technology.

Results

  • About $3 million raised for the company with Fowler's help, giving it the runway it was asking for.
  • A five-question method for testing a hard technical claim from close range, and the rule that came with it: closeness counts as diligence only when it produces a written page someone who was not in the room could check.
