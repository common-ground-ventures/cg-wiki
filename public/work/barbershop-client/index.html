A five-star barbershop: book without calling, then build the school

Work · Brand

Website, a phone line of its own and booking into Square for a five-star barbershop, then planning for the owner's barber school franchise.

Wiki/Work/A five-star barbershop

What it is

A barbershop with a five-star reputation, built one chair at a time by its owner. The back office already ran on Square, but the website was a generic landing page that could not take a booking, and every call to the shop rang the owner's personal cell, mid-haircut or after hours.

Callers who got no answer called the next listing. The busier the shop got, the more calls it missed, because the hands that earned the stars were the hands that could not reach the phone.

What Common Ground did

  • Common Ground gave the shop a phone line of its own, so calls reached the business instead of the owner's pocket.
  • The new site quotes the price and time of each of the shop's nine services, so a customer can decide without calling.
  • Rather than add a second booking product, the site books straight into Square, the calendar the barbers already worked from, so nothing is re-keyed and nobody had to learn a new tool.
  • Common Ground's brand team put the brand on top once the systems worked.
  • Common Ground runs the site end to end, and caught and fixed a five-month stretch where the live code and what customers saw on several surfaces had drifted apart.
  • Once the shop booked itself, the work turned to the owner's plan for a barber school with a national franchise behind it. Each premise went to a primary source before it entered the model. The owner believed the affluent side of his metro had no barber school; the Arizona Barbering and Cosmetology Board's registry of licensed schools showed several. What none had was a premium, brand-led identity anchored to a name customers already trusted, and the model was rebuilt on that narrower, true claim.
  • The board's requirement of 1,200 clock hours, 7 to 9 months full-time, confirmed the owner's estimate. Every funding channel was placed in the year it actually opens for a new school: workforce funding near licensure, veterans' benefits after a federally fixed operating history, federal student aid after accreditation. The base case runs on cash, payment plans, private lending and workforce funding alone.
  • Common Ground priced the premium instead of assuming it: the owner's $10,000 target tuition sits at the midpoint of a $6,000 to $14,000 local range, so the name and the facility have to earn it. The brand anchors to the shop, and the building goes into a second-generation retail box about a third cheaper nearby.
  • The unit economics were built from real rent, instructor payroll and a conservative ramp across five revenue lines, including the public student-clinic floor. At 70 enrollments in a mature year the model shows $1,044,000 in revenue at a 37.4 percent operating margin.
  • That feasibility work was the first pass. The national franchise work, taking the concept past one campus, is underway now and stands on it.

Results

  • A shop customers can book without calling, on the Square calendar it already ran, with pricing quoted on all nine services.
  • A phone line that reaches the business and lets the owner keep cutting.
  • A school case built on the state board's own records that survived its own red team: $1,044,000 of modeled revenue at a 37.4 percent operating margin at 70 enrollments, with the constraint most likely to bind named up front, the owner's own hours on the floor.
  • A client relationship that grew from a website into a national franchise plan, underway now.
