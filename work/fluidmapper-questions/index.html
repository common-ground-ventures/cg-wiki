Fluidmapper: questions answered

Common Ground on Fluidmapper.

Questions about Fluidmapper, answered in the words of the people who did the work. The engagement itself is on its own page.

What did buyer conversations reveal that the original technical story left out?

Radiation safety. The original explanation did not mention it. Fluidmapper was pre-revenue when we came in, and we never factored it in. You find the holes in a narrative by talking with real buyers, and this was one. So we added it to the story for when it comes up: the isotopes, the tracer particles, which are very expensive, and the shielded box they travel in. The founder has deep experience here, and for a scientist safety practice is part of daily life, so it goes unsaid. Site safety is the same in construction. Our job is to say it for buyers. There was no claim to walk back, just a narrative learning from client feedback.

What happens when a buyer's request falls outside the standard proposal?

We are working one right now. A company wants to run mixing experiments in zero gravity, and the question is whether the setup can fly on a small rocket or satellite and what that does to the results. Nothing in the standard matrix covered that. It is new for everybody. Companies keep bringing us uses we never expected once they see what Fluidmapper measures. The reusable proposal is what lets us scope them fast: their spec goes into the experiment matrix, the number of conditions sets price and timeline together, and the statement of work stays the same. The scoping call here is what changes when the lab leaves the ground.

What did the proposal system change?

Turnaround. A proposal used to take weeks to reach a document the client could act on. Now we can be at the next step within a few hours, which speeds up the whole sales process. It also moved the bottleneck. The client can make a budget decision much faster, but most of them still need outside financing to fund the work, so the wait shifts back to the buyer. That is the real constraint now, and it sits with the buyer, not with us.

What stays out of the reusable system on purpose?

Three things. The experiment matrix is the client's: their vessel, their fluid, their conditions, and the number of conditions sets price and timeline together. The commercial terms come from the scoped conversation with that account, never from a default. And scope itself stays a conversation with the founder, so we do not put measurement limits in the template. What the template does fix is the shape: the statement of work, the assumptions, one signature block, and a credibility appendix that draws only from confirmed results, never a projection.

How were the technical deck and the safety and IP questions turned into buyer-led materials?

The flyers waited until the buyers were ranked. The founder asked for them after the conference, half common content and half tailored, reading like an engineering onboarding program, and the reader was a PhD engineer who discounts a whole document over one soft number. So every figure went into a claims file, confirmed or unconfirmed, and the unsourced case-study savings stayed fenced off; the flyers drew only from the confirmed column. Instead of another accuracy figure, the software flyer led with the file: a simulation engineer can open the measured result in his own tools, in the same format as his own simulation. Radiation safety and IP were holes we found by talking to real buyers, so the site plan put that answer in the first screen, before a competent reader hit the question.

Which buyer segment did the closed contracts come from?

Those contracts are confidential, and so is which segment each one came from, so we will not walk a buyer's path here. That is also the honest limit on the ranking. Which segment closed is the fact that would test it, and until that can be said, the order stands as a defensible judgment, not a validated one. We would rather say that plainly than let someone assume the ranking was proven.

What belongs to the Fluidmapper engagement?

One client, one engagement. The brand work was done under the Lip Loi name but contracted through Common Ground, so all of it belongs here. It ran from the sales deck, pitch, brand strategy and asset library through fluidmapper.com, the customer pricing model and go-to-market planning. After that came the trade-show booth collateral, four vertical flyers, a one-pager and FAQ program, the sales and website architecture, a target-company matrix and LinkedIn campaign, a go-to-market deep dive, a proposal system, and the gated sample-data package on the site.

Which piece helped turn a founder-led sales motion into one a full-time salesperson could carry?

The flyers we created. They define the different markets, what they are and how to talk to each one, because it is a different conversation for each of the four verticals.
