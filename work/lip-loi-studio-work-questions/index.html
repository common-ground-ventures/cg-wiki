Lip Loi Creative portfolio: questions answered

Questions about Lip Loi Creative portfolio, answered in the words of the people who did the work. The engagement itself is on its own page.

What followed once the Olympic champion's brand deck reached sponsors and corporate buyers?

He signed with a major outdoor clothing brand shortly after. We cannot name the brand. His speaking work took off too: he was booked out for the next 18 months with speaking engagements. That is the response we watch for. A brand deck's job is to make it easy for the right sponsors and buyers to say yes, and within a short window he had a clothing partner and a full calendar.

How was the deck cut down to what a buyer could book?

What mattered was getting them talking. The more specific they got, the more we learned about who they are beyond what the public sees. Then we cut, round by round, down to the story buyers could book.

What commercial outcome followed the golf creator's brand strategy?

Within about four months he signed with a different golf club company than anyone expected, and with an up-and-coming clothing company. Because he is an influencer, leaving the golf team he had been with was a big hoopla. How much do we credit to the positioning? Honestly, you cannot fully separate it. That is the old branding versus marketing versus sales argument: what closed the deal? What we can say is the positioning gave him a narrative he did not have. His old group was a team brand. We made it about his own brand, and that was certainly part of it.

How was the one-garment discipline held when the broader apparel line was also part of the ambition?

By starting with one thing at a time, and by picking the right one first. The golf polo is the most important piece a golfer wears. That is where the logos go. It is what has to fit well and perform best. It is where the tech fabric, the gussets and the seaming matter, because they either free up the swing or restrict it. So the polo came first, and it had to be right. Pants, shorts, caps and tees could come after. One at a time.

How was responsibility divided on the golf apparel deck?

We set the direction and the story, and the studio did the craft. The call we made before anything got designed was one thing at a time, and the golf polo first.

What was made visible so the apparel work could not drift back into generic visual branding?

We made the players show us. We had our golfers bring in their favorite shirt, and about half of them brought the same one, an older golf polo from a major athletic brand that we owned too. It came down to the cut: where the seams sat, the gussets, the stretch through the chest and shoulders, so it moved with the body. The cut makes it easier to play.

What changed in the final specification after the garment comparison?

Pricing, really. The last changes were about which materials we could swap for cheaper versions. We rely heavily on the way the garment is cut and functions, but we were trying to keep it to a price point that made sense. One thing we cut was the anti-wicking spray they put on a lot of these fabrics, and it is kind of worthless anyway, because if it is done poorly it washes out on its own.

What changed when the capabilities deck went into the market?

The deck was built to sell our labor. When we hit a gap between projects we can come in lean and fast, and that keeps our crews busy so we do not lose them between jobs. It got us connected with Habitat for Humanity here. The best thing that came from it was a takeover. A general contractor downtown had trades walk off a large new steakhouse at the convention center, six rooms with a bar and a sushi area. They saw the deck, called us about the labor gap, and we took the project over and finished it. Takeovers are common for us.

When trades walked off a job, what had to be assessed before taking responsibility for finishing it?

You have to write a very detailed work scope first. In this case that was not enough. What had been done before was too complex for us to take ownership of, because nobody could know which work was good and which was not. We usually do fixed bids. This one we ran all time and materials. We sent our people in on time and materials right away, managed by our site superintendent on site. That was the only way to engage with it: the client got crews moving immediately, and we never had to guarantee work we did not do.

How quickly were the crews on site after the call?

Right away. That was the point of the deck. We sell our labor into the gaps between our own projects, so when the call came we had crews who needed the work and could come in lean and fast. We did not stop to price a fixed bid on work nobody could see into. We sent the crews in on time and materials, managed by our site superintendent, and started. We do not have an exact day count to give you. What we can tell you is the crews were ours, they were available, and that is the whole value of the offer.
